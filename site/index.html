The choice

Blue pill, or glass pill.

A commercial VPN asks you to swap one stranger for another: your café's router for a company's exit node, shared with thousands, run on a privacy policy you cannot read the code of. Megamesh is the other pill — the one you can see through. Your server, your keys, one admin, every line on GitHub.

01 You run servers

The script

One idempotent bash file, every setting an environment variable, nothing interactive. Read it, then run it as root on a fresh VPS. Re-running is safe; it keeps your choices.

02 You have an AI agent

The prompt

Paste one prompt into any agent that can run shell commands over SSH — Perplexity Computer, Claude Code, Codex, your own. It carries the goal, the safety rules, and every trap we hit so the agent starts where we finished.

The rules matter: your key goes in first, passwords are never disabled before a key login has succeeded, secrets are never printed, and the agent asks before touching SSH or the firewall.

03 You just want a VPN

The Operator

Rent a VPS (any 1 GB box with CentOS Stream 9 will do), buy or reuse a domain, then give the Operator the IP, the root password, the domain and your email. It changes the root password to one you choose, loads the program, waits, and hands over: point your domain at the IP, log in with the admin account it created, scan a QR code.

Your root password transits through megamesh.sh for the minutes the install runs and is never stored. If that is not acceptable, doors 01 and 02 give the same result without us in the middle.

The agents

Three doors.
Everything else is a wall.

The public internet reaches exactly three ports. The firewall's default action is DROP, not reject: scanners get silence. To a port scanner, WireGuard does not even exist until it shows a valid handshake.

0failed SSH logins on the reference server in 25 days, before Megamesh. After: a handful of banned addresses.
tcp/22 sshKey-only once a key exists, no forwarding, 3 tries. fail2ban bans for 24 h, doubling to 30 days.
udp/51820 wgWireGuard. Silent to anything that is not a valid handshake.
tcp/443 httpsCaddy with a Let's Encrypt certificate, serving the wg-easy admin. Optionally VPN-only: outsiders get redirected to a URL of your choice.
everything elseDropped. No port 80, no admin port, no Docker ports leaking through — only two are ever published.
icmpPing and the path-MTU types only, so the tunnel never stalls on big packets.

Inside the box

  • wg-easy v15 — peers, QR codes, stats, 2FA on the admin. Runs with NET_ADMIN only.
  • Caddy 2 — automatic TLS over 443 (no port 80), HSTS, strict headers.
  • firewalld — the three-door policy, bound to the interface that carries your default route.
  • fail2ban — aggressive sshd jail, escalating bans.
  • topgrade — weekly, pinned and checksum-verified: packages and container images, then a health check; a bad image is rolled back before you notice.
  • Swap guard, sysctl hardening, log rotation — the boring parts that keep a 1 GB box alive.
  • Unattended admin account — created before port 443 is reachable, so nobody can race you to the setup wizard.

Dailydnf-automatic / unattended-upgrades apply package updates while you sleep.

There is no spoon

What this is not.

Do not try to bend the threat model. That is impossible. Instead, only try to realise the truth: a personal VPN changes who can see your traffic, not whether anyone can.

Not a privacy service

Your VPS provider sees your traffic leave their network. Megamesh hides you from the café Wi-Fi and your ISP, not from a subpoena.

Not multi-tenant

One admin, your devices. wg-easy supports many peers, but the hardening assumes a single trusted operator.

Not finished by the Operator

Door 03 changes your root password but leaves SSH on a password until someone installs a key, and leaves 2FA and VPN-only mode for you to switch on. Have a techie friend spend twenty minutes with the hardening notes; it is already a good box before that.

Not tested everywhere

CentOS Stream 9 on a 1 vCPU / 1 GB VPS is verified end to end. Rocky, Alma, Debian and Ubuntu take their own code paths and have not been run. The repository says exactly what has and has not been exercised.

The hard line

Every exit is a phone booth.

Once your devices are on the tunnel, everything you do leaves from one address — your server's. That is the point, and it is also a fact other people can use: a friend can allow-list you, your own boxes can trust you, a rate limit can make an exception. The author publishes his:

Hostnamepaulfleury.network IPv4151.243.225.214 Reverse DNSforward-confirmed Machine-readableegress.json

Verify it rather than trust it: dig +short A paulfleury.network and dig +short -x 151.243.225.214 must agree. It identifies a server, not a person — keep authentication in front of whatever you allow-list.
